# Service Business Meta Modelling — Discussion Document

**Project:** GenderSense (GSL)
**Date:** 10 March 2026
**Status:** Working draft for discussion — not yet reflected in the SysML model

---

## 1. Purpose and Intent

This document proposes a meta modelling approach for GenderSense, addressing three related goals:

- **Make the business model explicit and visible** alongside the business system model, so that the two can be developed iteratively, with each informing the other.
- **Define a generic Business Meta Model** that captures the structural, functional, and dynamic features of a business model for any service-oriented business — the "what and why" of the business: its value proposition, financial logic, resource strategy, and strategic direction.
- **Define a generic System Meta Model** (deferred, to be extracted from the maturing GSL system model) that captures the structural, functional, and dynamic features of a business system for any service-oriented business — the "how" of the business: its processes, platform, operations, knowledge, and governance mechanisms.

These are two distinct meta models, not one. A business model and a business system model are different things — the business model describes the strategic logic of the enterprise (what value is offered, to whom, how revenue is generated, what resources are needed); the business system model describes the operational machinery that implements that logic (processes, technology, data, workforce management). Maintaining this distinction is essential because it allows the two to be iterated independently — a change to the pricing model is a business model change that may or may not require system changes, while a platform technology decision is a system change that should not alter the business model.

The two meta models are connected by explicit, visible mappings. A ServiceOffering in the business model maps to one or more ServiceProcesses in the system model. A RevenueStream maps to billable events generated by the delivery system. A ResourceType in the business model maps to workforce, equipment, or platform elements in the system model. These mappings are where the iterative back-and-forth between business model and business system happens — and making them structural (rather than implicit) is a core benefit of the approach.

The intended benefits are: completeness checking (has every concern been addressed?), modular exploration (can I substitute this component and trace the consequences?), stress-testing (does this business model variant work financially and operationally?), and communicability (can I discuss this with a non-technical colleague, an investor, or a clinical advisor by pointing at a visible structure rather than narrating from memory?).

Both meta models are pitched at the level of "any business that sees itself primarily as serving customer need" — the same scope as lean service systems thinking. Healthcare specifics arise only when the GSL models instantiate the generic abstractions.

## 2. Conceptual Framework

### 2.1 The Five Concerns of a Service Business

Any service business, regardless of sector, must address five orthogonal but interconnected concerns. These are not layers or stages — they coexist and interact continuously.

The five concerns span both the business model and the business system model, but each concern has a natural primary home. Service Concept and Financial Model are primarily business model concerns. Service Delivery System is primarily a business system concern. Resource and Capability Model and Governance and Adaptation each straddle both — the business model asks "what resources does this strategy require?" while the system model asks "how are those resources managed operationally?" This overlap is where the two models meet and where the mappings between them are most important.

**Service Concept** — What value is delivered, to whom, and why it is worth paying for. This encompasses the value proposition, the customer segments served, the differentiation claim, and the promise made to each segment. It answers: "why does this business exist, and for whom?"

**Service Delivery System** — How the value is actually produced and delivered. This encompasses the processes, pathways, and workflows that transform inputs (customer need, clinical data, professional expertise) into outputs (resolved need, improved health, delivered service). It answers: "how do we do what we do?"

**Resource and Capability Model** — What resources and capabilities are required to operate the delivery system, and how they are acquired, developed, and allocated. Resources include people (by role and competence), estate and facilities, equipment, technology, licences, time, and money. Capabilities are the organised combinations of resources that produce service outcomes. It answers: "what do we need in order to do what we do?"

**Financial Model** — How money flows through the business. Revenue streams (how income is generated from service delivery), cost structure (how resources consume money), unit economics (the relationship between a unit of service delivered and its financial impact), and the resulting business viability. It answers: "does this work financially?"

**Governance and Adaptation** — How the business is regulated, governed, and evolved. Regulatory constraints (external standards that must be met), internal governance (quality, safety, ethics, risk management), strategic direction (where the business is heading), and learning mechanisms (how the business improves over time). It answers: "are we doing it right, and are we getting better?"

### 2.2 Relationships Between the Five Concerns

The concerns are not independent. The key structural relationships are:

- The **Service Concept** defines what the **Service Delivery System** must produce.
- The **Service Delivery System** consumes **Resources and Capabilities** to operate.
- **Resources and Capabilities** have costs, which flow into the **Financial Model**.
- The **Service Delivery System** generates revenue events, which flow into the **Financial Model**.
- **Governance and Adaptation** constrains all four other concerns (regulatory limits on what can be offered, how it can be delivered, who can deliver it, and what can be charged).
- The **Financial Model** constrains what **Resources and Capabilities** can be acquired, which constrains what the **Service Delivery System** can do, which constrains what **Service Concept** can be realised.
- **Governance and Adaptation** includes the learning loop: outcomes from service delivery feed back into service concept refinement and delivery system improvement.

This creates a circular dependency that is not a defect — it is the reality of running a business. The meta model makes the circle visible and navigable rather than implicit and surprising.

### 2.3 Modularity Principle

Each concern is decomposed into components. Components have:

- **Defined interfaces** — what they require from and provide to other components.
- **Substitutability** — a component can be replaced with an alternative that satisfies the same interfaces, allowing exploration of business model variants.
- **Varying levels of coupling** — loose coupling at higher abstraction levels (the Service Concept doesn't dictate platform technology choices), tighter coupling at lower levels (a specific clinical pathway requires specific clinical competencies).

This is the "loose fit at high abstraction, tight fit at low levels" principle from the original discussion. The meta model defines the interface contracts; the concrete model implements them.

### 2.4 Activity Awareness — A Cross-Cutting Foundation

Beneath and across all five concerns lies a foundational requirement: the business must be aware of every unit of activity that occurs within it, whether that activity is system-implemented or human-implemented, whether it is directly billable or not.

This matters because activity is the common currency that connects the five concerns. The Service Concept promises value; the Service Delivery System produces it through activities. Resources are consumed by activities. The Financial Model can only produce meaningful projections if it can see the full activity picture — not just the billable clinical encounters, but also the service-enabling activities (case preparation, inter-professional communication, platform maintenance), governance activities (audit, compliance, policy review), development activities (CPD, recruitment, service design), and overhead activities (administration, facilities management, insurance renewals). If any category of activity is invisible, the financial model underestimates cost, the resource model underestimates demand, and the capacity model produces false confidence.

Activity awareness is therefore not a feature of any single concern — it is a cross-cutting foundation that all five concerns depend on. It is treated here as a foundational concept rather than a sixth concern because it does not answer its own question about the business; rather, it provides the observational substrate that enables the other five concerns to answer theirs accurately.

**Activity taxonomy.** Every activity in the business falls into one of five categories:

- **Service delivery activity** — directly produces value for a customer. Visible to the customer, typically the basis of billable events. Examples: conducting an assessment, reviewing blood results, delivering a coaching session.
- **Service-enabling activity** — supports service delivery but is not directly visible to the customer. Essential for quality and continuity. Examples: case preparation, MDT discussion, clinical correspondence, shared care liaison with GPs.
- **Governance activity** — satisfies compliance, quality, and safety requirements. Mandated by regulation, professional standards, or internal governance. Examples: clinical audit, significant event analysis, CQC compliance, GDPR record-keeping, professional appraisal.
- **Development activity** — builds future capability. Invests current resource to create or improve future capacity, skills, or systems. Examples: CPD, platform development, service design, recruitment, staff training.
- **Overhead activity** — necessary operational activity that is neither directly value-producing nor capability-building. Examples: financial administration, insurance renewals, premises management, general correspondence.

These categories are not rigid boundaries — a single activity might serve multiple purposes (a clinical audit both satisfies governance and generates learning that drives development). The taxonomy exists to ensure completeness of coverage, not to force artificial classification. The important thing is that nothing falls through the gaps.

**Progressive elaboration.** Activity awareness operates at multiple levels of granularity, and different parts of the business model and system model can operate at different levels simultaneously:

- **Envelope level** — total activity hours per category estimated as a lump. "We estimate 20% of clinician time is spent on service-enabling activities." Suitable for early-stage business model exploration where precision is neither possible nor necessary.
- **Category level** — activity broken down by type within each category, with approximate proportions and durations. "Service-enabling activities break down as: case preparation (8%), clinical correspondence (5%), MDT discussion (4%), shared care liaison (3%)." Suitable for more detailed resource planning and financial modelling.
- **Tracked level** — individual activities are recorded by the system as discrete events with participants, duration, resource consumption, and category classification. This is what the business system provides when fully operational. The existing GSL pathway model already operates at this level for clinical service delivery activities (every action step is a modelled, observable event); the goal is to extend this coverage progressively to service-enabling, governance, development, and overhead activities.

Different parts of the business can operate at different granularity levels at the same time. Clinical service delivery might be at the tracked level from day one (because the pathway model already generates observable events). Governance activity might be at the category level (estimated time per governance process type). Overhead might remain at the envelope level until there is a reason to be more precise. Over time, granularity increases where it adds value — typically where a cost turns out to be larger than expected, or where a resource constraint turns out to be tighter than assumed.

**Relationship to projections.** When running financial or resource projections driven by the model, every projection draws on activity data. At the envelope level, projections use estimated percentages and averages. At the tracked level, projections use actual recorded data. The cross-cutting nature of activity awareness means that a projection can mix granularity levels — using tracked data for clinical activities, category-level estimates for service-enabling activities, and envelope estimates for overhead — and produce a result that is as precise as the available data allows, with the imprecision explicitly visible rather than hidden.

## 3. Service Business Meta Model — Abstract Components

### 3.1 Service Concept

| Component | Description | Examples (generic) |
|---|---|---|
| **CustomerSegment** | A defined group of customers with shared needs, behaviours, and willingness to pay. Each segment has distinct needs and may require distinct value propositions. | "Self-referring individuals," "Corporate clients," "Referred patients" |
| **ValueProposition** | A specific promise of value to a specific customer segment. Defines what the customer gets, how it differs from alternatives, and why it matters to them. | "Faster access than NHS," "Integrated digital + clinical," "Specialist expertise" |
| **ServiceOffering** | A packaged, deliverable unit of service that realises one or more value propositions for one or more segments. Has a name, a scope, a price (or pricing model), and a set of included capabilities. | "Initial assessment package," "Ongoing monitoring subscription," "Coaching programme" |
| **Channel** | A means by which customers discover, access, and interact with the service. Channels have reach, cost, and conversion characteristics. | "Website + SEO," "GP referral network," "Community word of mouth" |
| **DifferentiationClaim** | An explicit statement of how this service differs from available alternatives, from the customer's perspective. | "Model-driven safety guarantees," "Patient portal with real-time pathway visibility" |

### 3.2 Service Delivery System

| Component | Description | Examples (generic) |
|---|---|---|
| **ServiceProcess** | A defined sequence of activities that produces a service outcome. Has inputs, outputs, participants, and governance requirements. The clinical pathway is a healthcare specialisation of this. | "Customer onboarding," "Service delivery episode," "Complaint resolution" |
| **ServiceEpisode** | A bounded instance of service delivery for a specific customer, with a defined start, progression, and end. | "A single course of treatment," "A consulting engagement," "A repair job" |
| **ActivityStep** | A single activity within a service process. May be performed by a person, a system, or a combination. Has resource requirements, duration characteristics, and quality criteria. | "Conduct assessment," "Process lab results," "Deliver coaching session" |
| **EntityLifecycle** | A stateful object that progresses through defined states as a result of service delivery activities. | "Order lifecycle," "Referral lifecycle," "Prescription lifecycle" |
| **ServiceOutcome** | A defined, measurable result of service delivery. May be customer-reported, professionally assessed, or system-measured. | "Customer satisfaction score," "Clinical target achieved," "Time to resolution" |
| **HandoffPoint** | A defined point where responsibility transfers between participants, systems, or organisations. Handoffs are a primary source of quality and safety risk. | "Referral to external specialist," "Transition to shared care," "Shift handover" |

### 3.3 Resource and Capability Model

| Component | Description | Examples (generic) |
|---|---|---|
| **ResourceType** | A category of resource required to operate the service delivery system. Each type has acquisition characteristics (hire, buy, rent, subscribe), capacity constraints, and cost profiles. | "Clinical personnel," "Technology platform," "Physical premises," "Professional licence" |
| **ResourceInstance** | A specific resource available to the business. Has a capacity (hours, throughput, seats), a cost (fixed, variable, or blended), and availability constraints. | "Dr Smith, 3 days/week," "EHRbase CDR instance," "Clinic room A" |
| **Capability** | An organised combination of resources that produces a defined service function. Capabilities bridge the gap between raw resources and service delivery — you don't deliver care with "a doctor" and "a system"; you deliver it with "the ability to conduct a hormone therapy monitoring review," which requires specific combinations of personnel, technology, data, and time. | "Prescribing capability," "Video consultation capability," "Laboratory ordering capability" |
| **CapacityModel** | The relationship between resource availability and service throughput. Defines how many service episodes or activities can be sustained with a given resource configuration. | "With 2 clinicians at 8 sessions/day, we can see 80 patients/week for routine monitoring" |
| **ResourceConstraint** | A constraint on resource availability or use that affects service delivery. May be temporal (clinician availability), regulatory (scope of practice), financial (budget limit), or contractual (licence terms). | "Prescribing clinician must hold relevant qualification," "Maximum 40 active patients per clinician" |

### 3.4 Financial Model

| Component | Description | Examples (generic) |
|---|---|---|
| **RevenueStream** | A mechanism by which income is generated. Each stream has a source (which customer segment), a trigger (what event or condition generates revenue), a pricing model (per-episode, subscription, tiered, etc.), and projected volume. | "Per-assessment fee," "Monthly monitoring subscription," "Coaching package" |
| **CostDriver** | A structural factor that generates cost. Cost drivers are linked to resources and capabilities — the cost of delivering a service is the cost of the resources consumed by the capabilities exercised. | "Clinician time per episode," "Platform hosting per active user," "Insurance premium per clinician" |
| **UnitEconomics** | The financial profile of a single unit of service delivery (typically one service episode or one customer over a period). Revenue per unit minus cost per unit equals margin per unit. This is the fundamental building block of financial viability. | "Revenue per HRT initiation episode: £X. Cost: £Y (clinician time £A, lab costs £B, platform £C, overhead £D). Margin: £Z." |
| **FinancialProjection** | A structured model of how revenue, cost, and margin scale with volume, time, and business model parameters. Not a spreadsheet — a set of structural relationships that can be parameterised and explored. | "At N patients, revenue = f(N, pricing), cost = g(N, resource model), margin = revenue - cost" |
| **PricingModel** | The logic that determines what a customer pays for a service offering. May be simple (fixed fee) or complex (tiered, outcome-linked, subscription with usage). | "Flat fee per assessment," "Monthly subscription with included consultations," "Sliding scale based on income" |

### 3.5 Governance and Adaptation

| Component | Description | Examples (generic) |
|---|---|---|
| **RegulatoryRequirement** | An externally imposed standard, regulation, or obligation that the business must meet. Has a source (regulator, law, professional body), a scope (what it applies to), and compliance criteria. | "CQC fundamental standards," "GDPR data protection," "GMC prescribing standards" |
| **GovernanceProcess** | An internal process for ensuring quality, safety, compliance, and ethical operation. Distinct from service delivery processes — governance processes oversee and assure service delivery. | "Clinical audit cycle," "Incident reporting and learning," "Policy review" |
| **RiskRegister** | A structured record of identified risks to the business, with assessment, mitigation, and monitoring. | "Clinical risk from prescribing errors," "Financial risk from low conversion," "Reputational risk from data breach" |
| **LearningMechanism** | A structured means by which the business improves over time, based on outcome data, incident analysis, customer feedback, and operational metrics. | "Outcome-driven pathway refinement," "Significant event analysis," "Customer satisfaction review" |
| **StrategicObjective** | A defined business goal with a timeframe, success criteria, and relationship to other objectives. Provides the intentional direction that the other concerns serve. | "Achieve 200 active patients within 18 months," "Establish shared care with 50 GP practices," "Reach break-even by month 24" |

### 3.6 Activity Awareness (Cross-Cutting)

| Component | Description | Examples (generic) |
|---|---|---|
| **ActivityType** | A defined category of activity within the business. Every activity has a type, and every type belongs to one of the five taxonomy categories (service delivery, service-enabling, governance, development, overhead). Types have expected duration ranges, resource requirements, and frequency characteristics. | "Clinical consultation," "Case preparation," "Clinical audit session," "Platform sprint," "Insurance renewal" |
| **ActivityRecord** | A recorded instance of an activity. At the tracked level, the system produces these for every observable activity. At the category and envelope levels, they are estimated aggregates rather than individual records. Each record captures: what type, who participated, how long, what resources were consumed, which concern(s) it serves. | "Dr Smith conducted a 45-minute monitoring review for patient X on 10 March" |
| **ActivityBudget** | A planned allocation of activity volume by type, period, and resource. The activity-level equivalent of a financial budget — how much activity of each type do we expect or intend to perform? Connects directly to resource capacity planning and financial projections. At the envelope level, this is "clinicians spend approximately 20% of time on service-enabling activities." At the tracked level, it is a detailed schedule. | "Q1 plan: 200 monitoring reviews, 50 assessments, 40 hours governance, 80 hours development" |
| **ActivityGranularity** | The level of detail at which activity is tracked for a given activity type or business area. Explicitly recorded so that projections can account for the precision of their inputs. One of: envelope, category, or tracked. | "Clinical service delivery: tracked. Governance: category. Overhead: envelope." |
| **ActivityCostAllocation** | The mapping from an activity record (or aggregate) to its financial cost. Every activity consumes resources; this component makes that consumption visible to the financial model. At the envelope level, costs are allocated by percentage of total resource cost. At the tracked level, costs are derived from actual resource consumption per activity. | "Monitoring review costs £85 (30 min clinician time @ £100/hr + £10 lab interface + £25 platform/overhead allocation)" |

### 3.7 Scenario Modelling (Projection Mechanics)

The components in sections 3.1–3.6 provide the vocabulary for describing a business model. Scenario Modelling provides the mechanics for *driving* that vocabulary — computing projections, comparing alternatives, and stress-testing assumptions. Without this, the business model is a structured description; with it, the business model becomes an explorable, quantitative tool.

| Component | Description | Examples (generic) |
|---|---|---|
| **ScenarioDefinition** | A named, self-contained configuration of a business model variant. Selects which ServiceOfferings are active, sets all parameters (pricing, staffing, volumes), specifies growth assumptions, and configures resource levels. Each of the business model variants described in section 7 becomes a ScenarioDefinition. A scenario is a complete, evaluable specification — given a scenario, the projection engine can produce a time-series financial output without further input. | "Lean Clinical: assessment + HRT only, 1 clinician 0.5 FTE, per-episode pricing at £350/assessment + £150/monitoring, target 50 patients in 12 months" |
| **ProjectionParameter** | A named, typed variable within a scenario that can be set to a specific value or range. Parameters are the knobs that distinguish one scenario from another. Some parameters are scalar (price per assessment: £350), some are temporal (clinician FTE: 0.5 in months 1–6, 1.0 from month 7), some are derived from other model components (activity hours per patient per quarter, computed from pathway step durations and activity budgets). | "assessmentPrice: £350," "clinicianFTE: 0.5," "patientGrowthRate: 5/month," "overheadPercentage: 30%" |
| **GrowthAssumption** | A time-parameterised trajectory for a variable. Defines how a quantity changes over the projection period. Growth assumptions are the bridge between a point-in-time business model description and a time-series projection. Common shapes include: linear (constant rate of increase), stepped (discrete jumps at defined milestones), S-curve (slow start, rapid growth, plateau), and custom (arbitrary trajectory defined by a series of time-value pairs). | "Patient numbers: +5/month for months 1–12, +10/month for months 13–24" or "Patient numbers: S-curve from 0 to 200 over 24 months with inflection at month 9" |
| **ProjectionFormula** | An evaluable expression that computes a derived quantity from parameters, model components, and activity data. Formulas are the structural relationships that connect the business model components into a computable whole. Each formula has named inputs (drawn from parameters, other formulas, or model components), an expression, and a named output. Formulas can reference activity budgets at whatever granularity level is available — envelope estimates at one level of detail, tracked actuals at another. | "monthlyRevenue = (activeAssessmentPatients × assessmentPrice / assessmentDurationMonths) + (activeMonitoringPatients × monitoringFeePerQuarter / 3)" |
| **ProjectionTimeline** | The temporal structure over which a projection is computed. Defines the projection period (e.g. 24 months), the reporting interval (monthly, quarterly), and the time steps at which growth assumptions and formulas are evaluated. | "24-month projection, monthly intervals, evaluated at month-end" |
| **ProjectionOutput** | The time-series result produced by evaluating a ScenarioDefinition over a ProjectionTimeline. Contains computed values for each reporting interval: revenue (by stream), cost (by driver and activity category), margin, cumulative cash flow, resource utilisation, break-even indicators. Also captures the confidence profile — which inputs are at tracked level (high confidence), category level (moderate), or envelope level (low) — so that the precision of the output is transparent. | "Month 6: revenue £4,200, cost £5,800, margin -£1,600, cumulative cash -£12,400, clinician utilisation 62%, confidence: moderate (3 of 8 cost drivers at envelope level)" |
| **SensitivityParameter** | A projection parameter identified for sensitivity analysis — exploring how the projection output changes when this parameter is varied. Sensitivity analysis reveals which assumptions the business model is most dependent on. Each sensitivity parameter has a base value, a range (optimistic and pessimistic bounds), and the projection output's sensitivity to variation within that range. | "If patient growth rate varies from 3/month (pessimistic) to 8/month (optimistic), break-even shifts from month 18 to month 10" |
| **ScenarioComparison** | A structured side-by-side comparison of two or more ScenarioDefinitions, evaluated over the same ProjectionTimeline. Highlights where the scenarios diverge — different break-even points, different resource requirements, different risk profiles — and makes the trade-offs between variants visible and quantitative. | "Lean Clinical breaks even at month 14 with £30K investment. Full Platform breaks even at month 22 with £120K investment but reaches £15K/month margin vs £4K/month." |
| **PeriodActuals** | The actual financial and activity figures for a completed period, recorded against the same structure as the ProjectionOutput. Not a separate format — the same revenue streams, cost drivers, activity categories, and resource utilisation dimensions. This is what makes comparison between forecast and actual meaningful: the actuals decompose along the same dimensions as the projection, so that a shortfall can be attributed to specific causes rather than appearing as a single unexplained number. | "Month 6 actuals: revenue £3,100, cost £5,600, margin -£2,500. Revenue shortfall vs forecast: -£1,100 (3 new patients vs forecast 4, average assessment fee £550 vs forecast £600)." |
| **VarianceAnalysis** | A structured comparison of PeriodActuals against the ProjectionOutput for the same period, decomposed by source. Each variance is attributed to one or more causal factors — volume variance (fewer or more units than expected), price variance (different unit revenue or cost), mix variance (different proportions of service types), timing variance (activity occurring earlier or later than planned). The variance analysis is the primary feedback signal for business steering: it tells you not just that you're off plan, but why, and which assumptions need revisiting. | "Month 6 variance: -£1,100 revenue. Decomposition: volume variance -£800 (3 vs 4 new patients), price variance -£300 (£550 vs £600 assessment fee). Cost variance: +£200 favourable (lab costs lower than budgeted)." |
| **ForecastBaseline** | A versioned snapshot of a ScenarioDefinition's parameters at a specific point in time. The original baseline is version 1. When accumulated variance indicates that assumptions are no longer valid, a re-baseline creates a new version: updated parameters reflecting what has been learned from actuals, with the forward projection re-run from the current position. Previous baselines are preserved, enabling three-way comparison: original plan vs current forecast vs actuals. The re-baseline is a deliberate act — it represents a decision that the business model assumptions have materially changed, not just that one month was unusual. | "Baseline v1 (launch): 4 patients/month growth. Baseline v2 (month 8, re-baselined): 3 patients/month growth based on 8 months of actuals averaging 3.2. Forward projection re-run from current position of 22 active patients." |

### 3.8 Operational Steering: The Forecast–Actuals–Rebaseline Cycle

The components in section 3.7 support two distinct modes of use: **planning mode** (before operations begin, exploring scenarios and choosing a strategy) and **steering mode** (once operations are underway, tracking performance against plan and adapting).

In steering mode, the projection is a living instrument. The cycle is:

1. **Establish baseline.** A ScenarioDefinition is selected and its ProjectionOutput becomes the operational plan — the reference against which performance will be measured. This is ForecastBaseline version 1.

2. **Record actuals.** Each period (typically monthly), actual financial and activity figures are recorded as PeriodActuals against the same structure as the projection. Revenue by stream, cost by driver, activity volumes by category, resource utilisation — all in the same dimensions as the forecast.

3. **Analyse variance.** The VarianceAnalysis compares actuals to forecast for the period and cumulatively. Variances are decomposed by cause. The analysis answers: are we on track? If not, why not? Is the deviation a one-off or a trend?

4. **Update the forward forecast.** Based on actuals and variance analysis, the remaining projection periods are updated. This may be as simple as "actuals are tracking close to plan, no changes needed" or as significant as "patient acquisition is consistently 25% below plan, adjust the growth assumption for the remaining periods." Small adjustments update the current baseline's forward projection without creating a new version.

5. **Re-baseline when necessary.** When the accumulated evidence indicates that the original assumptions are materially wrong — not just noisy, but structurally incorrect — a re-baseline creates a new ForecastBaseline version. The parameters are updated to reflect what has been learned, and the full forward projection is re-run from the current position (current patient numbers, current revenue run rate, current cost structure). The previous baseline is preserved. This is a deliberate decision, not an automatic adjustment — it represents a conscious acknowledgement that the business model assumptions have changed.

6. **Compare across baselines.** At any point, three views are available: the original plan (what we expected when we started), the current forecast (what we now expect based on accumulated learning), and the actuals (what has actually happened). The gap between original plan and current forecast shows how much the business model assumptions have shifted. The gap between current forecast and actuals shows how well the updated assumptions are tracking.

This cycle is the operational counterpart of the strategic scenario comparison in section 7.3. Where scenario comparison asks "which business model should we pursue?", the forecast-actuals-rebaseline cycle asks "is the business model we chose actually working, and if not, what do we need to change?"

The progressive elaboration principle applies here too. In the early months, actuals may be recorded at a coarse level (total revenue, total cost, total patients). As the business and the system mature, actuals can be recorded at finer granularity — per-stream revenue, per-driver cost, per-category activity — and the variance analysis becomes correspondingly more informative.

**Architectural note.** The forecast-actuals-rebaseline cycle is not a standalone system to be built from scratch. It is structurally parallel to the self-knowledge architecture already elaborated in the GSL system model's Knowledge layer — the same pattern of goal projection, operational state query, gap analysis, deficit identification, and structured explanation, applied to business performance rather than clinical governance. Section 4.7 maps this connection in detail. The implication is that implementing operational steering is primarily an extension of existing infrastructure: the system that already knows whether every patient has their monitoring bloods on schedule can be extended to know whether revenue is tracking to plan, and to explain why or why not, using the same evaluation, inference, and explanation machinery.

## 4. Mapping to the GSL Business System Model

The existing GSL SysML v2 model maps onto this meta model as follows. This mapping shows where the existing model already covers the meta model's concerns and where there are gaps.

### 4.1 Service Concept → Currently Minimal

The existing model has `Enterprise::Strategy::BusinessModel` with two string attributes (`modelName`, `description`). The rich structure of customer segments, value propositions, service offerings, channels, and differentiation claims is not yet modelled. This is the primary gap that the new `BusinessModel` package would fill.

### 4.2 Service Delivery System → Well Developed

This is the strongest part of the existing model:

- **ServiceProcess** → `ServiceDelivery::ClinicalPathways` (action defs with two-layer pattern)
- **ServiceEpisode** → `ServiceDelivery::ClinicalEntities::Episode` (with full lifecycle state machine)
- **ActivityStep** → Action steps within pathway action defs (annotated with metadata)
- **EntityLifecycle** → `ServiceDelivery::ClinicalEntities` (Patient, Episode, Prescription, LabResult, Referral — all with state defs)
- **ServiceOutcome** → `Knowledge::OutcomeFramework` (OutcomeDefinition, MeasurementSchedule, OutcomeEvaluationResult)
- **HandoffPoint** → Implicit in pathway structure (e.g., `transitionToOngoingCare`) but not modelled as a first-class concept.

### 4.3 Resource and Capability Model → Not Yet Modelled

This is the second major gap. The model implies resources (pathway annotations reference `reviewerRole = "prescribing-clinician"`) but does not model resource types, instances, capabilities, capacity, or constraints as first-class elements. You can see *that* a prescribing clinician is needed, but not how many are available, what they cost, how their time is allocated, or what happens when capacity is exceeded.

### 4.4 Financial Model → Not Yet Modelled

The financial dimension is entirely absent from the SysML model. `Operations::Finance` has two use cases (`ProcessInvoice`, `ReconcilePayments`) — operational accounting, not business model economics. Revenue streams, cost drivers, unit economics, and financial projections are not represented.

### 4.5 Governance and Adaptation → Well Developed

- **RegulatoryRequirement** → `Enterprise::Regulation` (detailed requirement defs with satisfy relationships)
- **GovernanceProcess** → `ServiceDelivery::ClinicalGovernance` (audit, SEA, protocol review)
- **RiskRegister** → `Enterprise::Risk` (use cases, not yet structural)
- **LearningMechanism** → `Knowledge::LearningCycles` (pathway refinement, evidence review, change control)
- **StrategicObjective** → Not yet modelled (beyond the placeholder `Enterprise::Strategy`)

### 4.6 Activity Awareness → Partially Present, Not Explicit

The existing model has strong activity tracking for clinical service delivery — every action step in a pathway is a modelled event, and the Temporal orchestration layer produces observable workflow state for each step. The Knowledge layer's OperationalStateAggregator can query active, blocked, and failed workflows, giving a real-time picture of clinical service delivery activity.

However, service-enabling activities (case preparation, GP liaison, clinical correspondence), governance activities (audit, compliance), development activities (CPD, platform development), and overhead activities (administration, insurance) are not modelled as trackable events. They are either implicit (someone must prepare for a case, but the model doesn't represent this) or mentioned in package doc blocks without structural representation.

The gap is not that these activities are unknown — they are clearly understood — but that the model has no structural place for them. Adding cross-cutting activity awareness as a foundational concept (section 2.4) addresses this by providing the vocabulary and progressive elaboration levels needed to make all activity visible, starting at the envelope level for less-elaborated areas and progressing to tracked level as the system matures.

### 4.7 Scenario Modelling and Operational Steering → Architecturally Enabled by Self-Knowledge

The forecast-actuals-rebaseline cycle described in section 3.8 and the scenario modelling components in section 3.7 are not standalone capabilities that need to be built from scratch. They are a domain extension of the self-knowledge architecture already elaborated in `Knowledge::LogicEngine`.

The structural parallel is direct:

| Self-Knowledge (Clinical) | Operational Steering (Business) |
|---|---|
| **GoalProjector** projects goals from requirements, constraints, and outcome definitions | **ProjectionFormulas** project financial and activity goals from scenario parameters |
| **OperationalStateAggregator** queries Temporal, CDR, and platform services for current state | Extended aggregator queries financial systems, activity records, and resource utilisation for current actuals |
| **GapAnalyser** compares operational state against goal projections, produces Deficit records | **VarianceAnalysis** compares PeriodActuals against ProjectionOutput, produces structured variance decomposition |
| **Deficit** records what's wrong, the expected state, the actual state, and the remediation category | **Variance** records the forecast-actual gap, the causal factors, and whether re-baselining is warranted |
| **ExplanationTrace** provides structured "why" answers for clinicians, patients, and auditors | Extended explanation provides structured "why" answers for business steering — why is revenue below plan? which assumptions were wrong? |
| **InferenceEvaluator** (Tier 2) chains backward from a deficit through multiple contributing factors | Extended inference chains backward from a financial variance through volume, price, mix, and timing factors to root causes |
| **AssessmentOrchestrator** coordinates the five-layer SystemStateAssessment | Extended orchestrator coordinates a combined clinical + business state assessment |
| **SelfExplanationService** renders results for four audiences (clinician, patient, auditor, dashboard) | Extended service adds a fifth audience: business steering (founder, investor, board) |

The existing five-layer SystemStateAssessment structure accommodates this extension naturally:

- **Layer 1 (Structural)** — already reads the model manifest. Extend to include BusinessModel package structure (how many service offerings, how many revenue streams, how many scenario definitions).
- **Layer 2 (Operational)** — already queries Temporal, CDR, platform. Extend to query financial actuals and activity records. PeriodActuals become part of the operational snapshot.
- **Layer 3 (Goal)** — already projects from requirements, constraints, outcomes. Extend to project from ProjectionFormulas. A forecast figure for a given period is a goal.
- **Layer 4 (Gap)** — already compares layers 2 and 3 and produces Deficits. Financial and activity variances are deficits. A revenue shortfall is structurally identical to a clinical monitoring gap — both are cases where actual state falls short of expected state.
- **Layer 5 (Remediation)** — already classifies remediation as automatic, recommended, or advisory. Business variances follow the same pattern: some have deterministic responses (adjust next month's marketing spend), some are recommended (consider re-baselining growth assumptions), some are advisory (the business model may need structural revision).

This means that implementing the operational steering pattern is primarily an extension of existing infrastructure, not a parallel build. The ConstraintEvaluator pattern (resolve inputs from authoritative sources, evaluate against model-derived rules, produce structured explanations) applies identically — the difference is that the "rules" are projection formulas, the "inputs" are financial actuals, and the "explanations" are variance decompositions.

The inferential "why?" capability is particularly powerful here. When the system identifies that revenue is below forecast, Tier 1 evaluation can identify the immediate cause (fewer patients than projected). Tier 2 inference can chain deeper: are fewer patients due to lower acquisition (check marketing activity data), lower conversion (check assessment-to-initiation rate), higher churn (check monitoring disengagement), or a capacity constraint (check clinician utilisation against maximum)? Each link in the chain draws on the same authoritative data sources that the clinical self-knowledge already queries. And the goal-state projection capability — "what is needed to achieve the desired state?" — can run forward from the current position: "to reach break-even by month 18, given current actuals, the growth rate needs to increase from 3/month to 5/month, OR the assessment fee needs to increase from £550 to £700, OR overhead needs to decrease by 15%." These are the remediation options, presented with the same structural clarity as clinical deficit remediation.

### 5.2 Cross-References to Existing Packages

The BusinessModel package does not duplicate content from the existing six packages. Instead, it references them:

- `ServiceOfferings` reference `ServiceDelivery::ClinicalPathways` — each offering includes one or more pathways.
- `Capabilities` reference `Platform` capabilities and `ServiceDelivery` processes — each capability requires platform features and/or clinical processes.
- `CostStructure` references `Operations` — cost drivers map to operational processes and resource consumption.
- `RevenueStreams` reference `ServiceOfferings` — revenue is generated when offerings are delivered.
- `StrategicObjectives` reference `Enterprise::Strategy` — objectives are the operational expression of strategy.
- `ResourceConstraints` reference `Enterprise::Regulation` — some constraints arise from regulatory requirements.
- `BusinessModelVariants` compose alternative selections from the other sub-packages — "what if we offer only assessment and monitoring, not coaching?" is a variant that includes some ServiceOfferings and excludes others, with corresponding changes to ResourcePlanning and FinancialPlanning.
- `ActivityModel` references every other package — service delivery activities come from `ServiceDelivery` pathways, governance activities from `Enterprise::Regulation` and `ServiceDelivery::ClinicalGovernance`, service-enabling activities are inferred from pathway resource annotations, and development and overhead activities are referenced from `Operations`. At the tracked level, `ActivityModel::ActivityCostAllocations` consumes activity records produced by the system model (via `Foundation` types) and maps them to `FinancialPlanning::CostStructure`.

### 5.3 Relationship to the Existing Operations Package

There is an intentional division of responsibility between `BusinessModel::FinancialPlanning` and `Operations::Finance`:

- **BusinessModel::FinancialPlanning** — structural economics: how does this business model work financially? What are the revenue streams, cost drivers, unit economics? This is the bank-manager spreadsheet made structural.
- **Operations::Finance** — operational accounting: invoice processing, payment reconciliation, payroll, Xero integration. This is the day-to-day financial machinery.

Similarly, `BusinessModel::ResourcePlanning` is about structural resource needs (how many clinicians do we need for N patients?), while `Operations::People` is about operational HR (contracts, recruitment, appraisal).

## 7. Exploration Pattern: Business Model Variants as Scenarios

The core practical benefit of this structure is the ability to explore business model variants as evaluable scenarios. Each variant is not just a narrative description but a ScenarioDefinition with concrete parameters, growth assumptions, and projection formulas that produce quantitative outputs.

### 7.1 Example Variants (Summary)

**Variant A: "Lean Clinical"** — Assessment and hormone therapy only. No coaching, no community. 1 prescribing clinician (0.5 FTE), 1 admin (0.3 FTE), platform only. Per-episode pricing. Break-even target at 50 active patients.

**Variant B: "Full Platform"** — Assessment, hormone therapy, coaching, education, community, patient portal with identity and journaling. 2 clinicians, 1 coach, 1 community moderator, full platform development. Monthly subscription model. Target 200 active patients within 18 months.

**Variant C: "Consultancy + Platform Licence"** — GSL delivers clinical services AND licences the platform to other gender services. Dual revenue: clinical fees + platform licence/SaaS. Higher upfront investment, but platform revenue scalable independently of clinical capacity. Establish reference site, then licence to 3 additional services within 3 years.

### 7.2 Worked Example: Variant A as a ScenarioDefinition

To illustrate how the scenario modelling components work together, here is Variant A ("Lean Clinical") sketched as a concrete ScenarioDefinition. All figures are illustrative — the point is to show the structural relationships, not to assert specific prices or costs.

**Active ServiceOfferings:**

- InitialAssessment — comprehensive new patient assessment (2 sessions over 2 weeks)
- HormoneTherapyInitiation — from baseline bloods through to stable regimen (6–12 months)
- OngoingMonitoring — quarterly monitoring reviews once stable

**ProjectionParameters:**

| Parameter | Value | Source |
|---|---|---|
| assessmentFeePerPatient | £600 | PricingModels |
| monitoringFeePerQuarter | £150 | PricingModels |
| clinicianFTE | 0.5 (months 1–9), 0.8 (months 10–24) | ResourcePlanning |
| clinicianCostPerFTEPerMonth | £5,000 | ResourcePlanning |
| adminFTE | 0.3 | ResourcePlanning |
| adminCostPerFTEPerMonth | £1,800 | ResourcePlanning |
| platformCostPerMonth | £200 | ResourcePlanning |
| labCostPerBloodPanel | £45 | CostStructure |
| bloodPanelsPerInitiationEpisode | 5 | ServiceDelivery (derived from pathway) |
| bloodPanelsPerMonitoringQuarter | 1 | ServiceDelivery (derived from pathway) |
| overheadPercentage | 25% | ActivityModel (envelope level) |
| insurancePerMonth | £250 | CostStructure |

**GrowthAssumptions:**

| Variable | Trajectory |
|---|---|
| newPatientsPerMonth | Linear: 4/month for months 1–12, 6/month for months 13–24 |
| assessmentToInitiationConversion | 85% (fixed) |
| initiationToStableConversion | 90% over 9 months (average) |
| churnRate | 5% of stable patients per quarter |

**Key ProjectionFormulas (simplified):**

```
patientsInAssessment[m] = newPatientsPerMonth[m]
patientsInInitiation[m] = patientsInInitiation[m-1]
    + (patientsInAssessment[m] × assessmentToInitiationConversion)
    - patientsCompletingInitiation[m]
patientsStable[m] = patientsStable[m-1]
    + patientsCompletingInitiation[m]
    - (patientsStable[m-1] × churnRate / 3)

monthlyRevenue[m] = (patientsInAssessment[m] × assessmentFeePerPatient / 2)
    + (patientsStable[m] × monitoringFeePerQuarter / 3)

monthlyDirectCost[m] = (clinicianFTE[m] × clinicianCostPerFTEPerMonth)
    + (adminFTE × adminCostPerFTEPerMonth)
    + platformCostPerMonth
    + insurancePerMonth
    + (bloodPanelsThisMonth[m] × labCostPerBloodPanel)

monthlyOverhead[m] = monthlyDirectCost[m] × overheadPercentage

monthlyMargin[m] = monthlyRevenue[m] - monthlyDirectCost[m] - monthlyOverhead[m]
cumulativeCashFlow[m] = cumulativeCashFlow[m-1] + monthlyMargin[m]
clinicianUtilisation[m] = clinicianActivityHours[m] / (clinicianFTE[m] × availableHoursPerMonth)
```

**SensitivityParameters:**

| Parameter | Base | Pessimistic | Optimistic | Impact on break-even |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| newPatientsPerMonth (year 1) | 4 | 2 | 6 | ±6 months |
| assessmentFeePerPatient | £600 | £450 | £750 | ±3 months |
| clinicianCostPerFTEPerMonth | £5,000 | £6,000 | £4,000 | ±2 months |
| overheadPercentage | 25% | 35% | 20% | ±2 months |

This reveals that the Lean Clinical variant is most sensitive to patient acquisition rate — the difference between 2 and 6 new patients per month shifts break-even by a full year. Pricing sensitivity is secondary. This is the kind of insight that a structured, parameterised scenario produces and that a narrative business plan cannot.

### 7.3 Scenario Comparison

Once two or more variants are defined as ScenarioDefinitions, a ScenarioComparison evaluates them over the same ProjectionTimeline and produces a structured comparison:

| Metric | Lean Clinical | Full Platform | Consultancy + Licence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Initial investment required | ~£15K | ~£120K | ~£200K |
| Month to break-even (base case) | 14 | 22 | 30 |
| Monthly margin at month 24 | ~£4K | ~£15K | ~£25K |
| Maximum cumulative cash deficit | ~£18K | ~£140K | ~£220K |
| Clinician FTE required at month 24 | 0.8 | 2.0 | 1.5 + support team |
| Active patients at month 24 | ~65 | ~180 | ~80 (own) + 3 licensees |
| Revenue diversification | Low (single stream) | Medium (clinical + subscription) | High (clinical + SaaS) |
| Sensitivity to patient acquisition | Very high | High | Moderate (licence revenue independent) |

All figures are illustrative. The value is not in the specific numbers but in the structural comparison — which variant requires more capital, which is more robust to acquisition shortfall, which has the higher ceiling, which fits the founder's current capacity and appetite for risk. These are the questions that the back-and-forth between business model and business system should help answer.

### 7.4 Relationship to the Four-Layer Architecture

The scenario modelling mechanics connect to the existing four-layer model-driven architecture:

- **Layer 1 (Authoritative SysML v2 model)** — ScenarioDefinitions, ProjectionParameters, GrowthAssumptions, and ProjectionFormulas are defined in the BusinessModel package as SysML v2 part defs with typed attributes. The model is the source of truth for the business model structure.
- **Layer 2 (Python-generated scaffolding)** — A generator reads the ScenarioDefinitions and ProjectionFormulas from the model and produces TypeScript or Python projection code — functions that take parameter values and produce time-series outputs. This parallels the existing constraint evaluator generation pattern.
- **Layer 3 (Hand-written implementation)** — The generated projection scaffolding is augmented with presentation logic (charts, comparison tables, export to spreadsheet format for sharing with advisors or investors).
- **Layer 4 (Configurable infrastructure)** — Scenario parameters are configurable at runtime. A simple UI (or even a CLI tool) allows parameter values to be adjusted and projections re-run without modifying the model or regenerating code. This is the "what-if" exploration interface.

Initially, the projection engine can be a hand-written Python or TypeScript module that implements the formulas directly — getting working projections quickly. The generation pipeline can be added later when the formula patterns stabilise, following the same incremental approach used for the clinical constraint evaluators.

## 9. Simulation: Practising the Business Before Running It

The epistemological progression through sections 2–8 follows a natural arc: what is the current state? what was the past state? what should have been, and did we get it? what might the future hold? what alternative futures could we choose? Simulation extends this arc one further step: what would it be like to *experience* a future, in enough detail to learn from it, before committing real resources?

Simulation differs from projection in a fundamental way. A projection aggregates — it tells you "at month 12, you'll have approximately 50 patients generating approximately £X revenue." A simulation instantiates — it generates 50 individual simulated patients, each arriving at a specific time with specific characteristics, flowing through the pathway model, consuming specific resources, encountering specific decision points, generating specific financial events. The aggregate numbers may be similar, but the simulation reveals emergent behaviour that aggregation hides: bottlenecks, queuing effects, resource contention, timing coincidences, cascade failures, and the human experience of operating the service under realistic conditions.

The architecture described in this document brings simulation within reach because the essential components already exist or are planned. What follows describes the simulation capability as a future workstream, identifying the key design concerns.

### 9.1 Simulation Data Generation

The simulation requires a generative model that produces realistic input data:

**Patient generation.** Simulated patients are created according to configurable parameters: arrival rate (drawn from the GrowthAssumptions in a ScenarioDefinition), segment distribution (proportion of self-referral vs GP referral vs other), clinical profile distribution (hormone therapy candidates, assessment-only, coaching-only), and demographic characteristics relevant to pathway behaviour (age range, baseline clinical values, preference distributions). The generator must produce patients that are statistically plausible and clinically coherent — a simulated patient's baseline blood results should be consistent with their clinical profile, not random.

**Event generation.** External events that the real system would receive from outside — lab results returning, GP responses to shared care requests, patient cancellations, pharmacy dispensing confirmations — must be generated with realistic timing and content. Lab results should arrive after a configurable delay with values drawn from clinically plausible distributions. Some patients should miss appointments. Some GPs should decline shared care. The simulation's value depends on these events reflecting the messiness of reality, not just the happy path.

**Environmental and context starter sets.** A simulation run needs an initial state: how many patients are already in the system at what pathway stages, what resources are available, what the current financial position is. Starter sets define these initial conditions, enabling different simulation scenarios — "start from empty" (new service launch), "start from steady state" (established service exploring a change), or "start from a specific historical position" (replaying from a known point with different decisions).

### 9.2 Workflow Execution Under Simulation

The pathway action flows in `ServiceDelivery::ClinicalPathways` and their Temporal orchestration layer counterparts define the processes through which simulated patients flow. In simulation mode:

**Automated workflow progression.** In the live system, workflows pause at signal wait points (waiting for a clinician to complete a review, waiting for lab results). In simulation, these waits are resolved automatically by the event generator, with configurable delays that reflect realistic timing distributions. The workflow engine doesn't know or care whether it's processing a real patient or a simulated one — it executes the same pathway logic, evaluates the same constraints, triggers the same state transitions.

**Decision point resolution.** At clinical review gates and decision table evaluations, the simulation needs a decision-making agent. This could range from a simple rule-based responder (always follow the decision table recommendation) to a more sophisticated model that introduces realistic clinical variation (occasionally override the recommendation, sometimes delay a decision, occasionally make a suboptimal choice). The level of decision-making sophistication can be configured per simulation run depending on what's being explored.

**Resource contention.** Simulated activities compete for the same finite resources defined in `BusinessModel::ResourcePlanning`. When two simulated patients both need a monitoring review in the same week and the clinician only has capacity for one, the simulation must queue the second — revealing wait times, scheduling pressure, and potential breaches of monitoring intervals that a projection would not surface.

### 9.3 Temporal Control

Simulation time must be decoupled from real time and controllable:

**Time compression.** A 24-month business scenario should be runnable in minutes, not months. The simulation clock advances at a configurable rate, with all temporal events (patient arrivals, lab result delays, monitoring intervals, payment cycles) scaled accordingly.

**Start, stop, pause, save, delete.** The simulation can be started, paused at any point to inspect the current state (how many patients at each pathway stage, what the resource utilisation looks like, what the financial position is), and resumed from where it paused. It can be stopped (terminating the run and producing final outputs). The current state can be saved as a named snapshot at any point — enabling the user to return to that exact position later, or to branch the simulation from that point with different parameters. Saved simulation states can be deleted when no longer needed. The full lifecycle of a simulation run is: create (from a ScenarioDefinition and starter set) → start → pause/resume as needed → save snapshots as desired → stop → review outputs. Saved states and completed run outputs persist independently of the running simulation.

**Variable time intervals.** Some simulation runs may benefit from fine-grained time steps (daily, to observe scheduling and resource contention effects). Others may use coarser steps (weekly or monthly, for faster execution of long-horizon scenarios). The time step should be configurable per run, with the understanding that finer granularity produces more realistic emergent behaviour but takes longer to execute.

**Checkpointing.** The simulation state at defined intervals (or on demand) is captured as a checkpoint — a complete snapshot from which the simulation can be restarted. This enables branching ("what if, at month 6, we had hired a second clinician instead of raising prices?") and reproducibility ("replay this exact scenario for demonstration purposes").

### 9.4 Simulation Reporting and Outcomes

The simulation produces the same data structures as the live system, which means the same reporting and analysis infrastructure applies:

**Periodic simulation reports.** At configurable intervals during the simulation run, automated reports are generated using the same structures as real operational reports: financial summaries (revenue, cost, margin, cash flow), activity summaries (volumes by category, resource utilisation), clinical governance summaries (constraint compliance, outcome tracking), and SystemStateAssessments via the Knowledge layer's self-knowledge infrastructure. These reports are structurally identical to what the live system would produce — the simulation is a rehearsal of the real reporting cycle.

**Variance against plan.** The simulation's outputs can be compared against the ProjectionOutput from the same ScenarioDefinition. This reveals where the projection's aggregate assumptions diverge from the simulated reality — typically where stochastic variation and resource contention produce different outcomes from smooth averages.

**Cumulative outcomes.** At the end of a simulation run, the full history is available for analysis: patient journeys from referral to stable care, financial trajectory from launch to the end of the simulation period, resource utilisation patterns, bottleneck identification, governance compliance rates, and clinical outcome distributions.

### 9.5 Purposes of Simulation

The simulation capability serves multiple purposes, each requiring different configurations:

**Learning and intuition-building.** The founder or clinical lead runs simulations to develop intuition about how the business behaves under different conditions. "What does it feel like to run a clinic with 40 active patients and one clinician?" is a question best answered by experiencing a simulated version, not by reading a spreadsheet.

**Stress-testing.** Deliberate adversity scenarios: what happens if patient acquisition drops by half for three months? What if a clinician is unexpectedly unavailable for six weeks? What if a regulatory change requires an additional governance process? The simulation reveals cascading effects that are invisible in static analysis.

**Demonstration.** A running simulation with a visual interface — patients flowing through pathways, resource utilisation gauges, financial dashboards updating in compressed time — is a compelling way to demonstrate the platform to potential investors, partners, clinical advisors, or licensees. It shows not just what the system does but how it behaves dynamically.

**Training.** Staff can practise operating the system — responding to alerts, making clinical decisions at review gates, managing scheduling pressure — in a simulated environment where mistakes are consequence-free. This is particularly valuable for onboarding new clinicians into the service model.

**Evaluation.** Comparing simulation outcomes across different business model variants provides a richer basis for decision-making than projection comparison alone. The simulation reveals qualitative differences (this variant feels manageable; that variant feels chaotic) alongside the quantitative ones.
